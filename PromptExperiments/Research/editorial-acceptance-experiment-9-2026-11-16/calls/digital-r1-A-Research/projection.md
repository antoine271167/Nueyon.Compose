### Facts

1. The author is the Product Manager for the Power of Attorney (POA) refunds service for the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG).
2. The author says their team built a digital and assisted digital service with no paper version.
3. The service was built to meet a need that the author says had not previously existed.
4. The service launched to the public in 12 weeks.
5. The service passed alpha and beta Service Standard assessments.
6. The OPG cannot make a profit from fees paid for POA applications.
7. The amount received each year in POA application fees must equal the cost of processing applications.
8. Fees are adjusted annually based on predictions of submitted applications.
9. Between 2013 and 2017, the amount received in fees exceeded processing costs.
10. Late the previous year, a team was set up to build a new service through which users could apply for a refund.
11. The planned service included a case management application to process claims and approve or reject them.
12. The source says OPG data was not always accurate.
13. The source says people tend not to update their details with OPG.
14. The source says this meant applicants could not be refunded directly.
15. The team was initially asked to deliver the service in a matter of weeks.
16. The author says this created huge pressure for everyone.
17. The team quickly discovered that delivery in that timeframe would not be possible if they wanted to create a quality service for users.
18. The team worked across three sites: London, Birmingham and Nottingham.
19. The source says the team had to balance business needs with user needs while the ministers in charge were changing during a ministerial reshuffle.
20. The author says the project required sensitivity in its approach to user research.
21. The research was limited to a specific user group: POA applicants from 2013 to 2017.
22. The author says this limited and specific user group made research trickier.
23. The team focused first on users applying for refunds, and this was the work taken to the alpha assessment.
24. Plans for the caseworker user came later.
25. The author says the team chose this order because, until applications were coming through, the caseworker user did not yet exist in this new service.
26. The author says the team also chose this order because, with limited time, it wanted to avoid going off course through lack of relevant knowledge or incorrect assumptions.
27. During private beta, the team started building the case management application while iterating the front end.
28. The team divided fortnightly sprints into two parts.
29. In the first week, the team tested, researched and accepted applications.
30. In the second week, the team suspended submissions to test, research and process applications.
31. The author says this sprint arrangement was key to building the service quickly while ensuring it met user needs.
32. The author’s stated advice is to be pragmatic and realistic about what can be achieved and in what timeframe, then be bold in presenting that to stakeholders and sometimes the rest of the team.
33. The author advises clearly outlining the pros and cons of all options when presenting to stakeholders or the team.
34. The team set a mission statement with measurable Key Performance Indicators (KPIs).
35. The author says the mission statement and KPIs helped support the plan and provide evidence to back decisions with stakeholders and in assessments.
36. The author says user research and evidence were key to achieving what they describe as the best outcome and value for all users and stakeholders.
37. The service had been available for five weeks when the source was written.
38. At that point, the service had received over 100,000 applications.
39. At that point, the source reports 92.6% user satisfaction.
40. At that point, the source reports an 80.2% completion rate.
41. The author recommends building services rather than form fillers.
42. The author says end-to-end services help internal and external users achieve their goals more efficiently.

### Facts
1. Lowell’s textile employment fell even as output rose through technological gains: total employment declined from 17,000 in 1895 to fewer than 14,000 in 1918. Owners knew mills were aging and noncompetitive, but chose not to modernize Lowell operations; some moved elsewhere or invested profits in Southern plants. (Source: “There were early signs”; “mill owners knew as early as the 1890s”)
2. Closures began before the Depression: Bigelow departed in 1914 and Middlesex Mills stopped production in 1918; a wave followed in 1926, including Hamilton’s receivership and Appleton’s move to the South. By the mid-1930s, only Merrimack, Lawrence, and Boott of the first large mills remained in operation. (Source: “As early as World War I”; “By the mid-1930s”)
3. By 1936, textile employment had fallen to 8,000. Many mills were empty or used by small manufacturers, while mill complexes were demolished or reduced; the account says parts of Lowell “looked like a war ravaged city.” (Source: “The Depression came early to Lowell”)
4. The account describes unemployment and hardship through workers’ recollections: one family ate dandelion greens and hard bread and sometimes sold possessions for meat; children left school for scarce work. Workers could arrive at the mill only to be sent home without assurance of work the next day. (Source: Depression accounts)
5. In the late 1930s, the mills’ “stretch-out” assigned workers more machines for the same wage. Merrimack worker Yvonne Hoar recalled dangerous, noisy conditions, doubled machines, and pay of $13 a week. Women helped organize a union in 1938; after wage and condition demands were rejected, workers struck. The owners capitulated after seven weeks amid approaching war and federal pressure for cloth, and workers returned. (Source: “As the few remaining large mills”; Hoar recollection; “When a union was formed in 1938”)
6. World War II temporarily raised employment and wages: military-contract firms competed with mills for workers; the account gives mill starting pay rising from $13 a week in 1938 to $29 in 1943, and average Remington earnings of $37 for a 48-hour week in 1943. After the war, orders fell; Boott and Merrimack closed in the 1950s, and urban renewal in the 1960s removed the latter’s mills and boardinghouses and Little Canada. (Source: “World War II quickened Lowell’s economy”; “When the war ended in 1945”)
7. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens devised a revitalization strategy involving education and the local economy, working with urban planners and historians on redevelopment based on the city’s architectural and cultural heritage. Their proposals included a historical park presenting the city as a “living museum.” The city council endorsed the idea in 1972; Lowell Heritage State Park emerged in 1974, Lowell National Historical Park in 1978, and the Lowell Historic Preservation Commission was created in 1978 to assist park development, preservation of downtown buildings and canals, and related cultural programs. (Source: “In the 1960s”; “Pragmatic alliances”)
8. The account identifies other contributors to the 1970s revival: the University of Lowell formed in 1975 through the union of Lowell Technological Institute and Lowell State College, with a regional economic-development mission; Wang Laboratories arrived in the mid-1970s, and many hoped it would provide a new technological future. (Source: “Other factors in the 1970s”)
9. The account reports that in the late 1970s and early 1980s, employment rose as business expanded and more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated and put to new uses; visitors returned, and Lowell was described as a model of historic preservation and urban revival. (Source: “The late 1970s and the early 1980s”)
10. That revival did not continue uninterrupted: by the late 1980s the regional economy had cooled, the computer industry was tightening its belt, and companies were closing or relocating. Wang faced strong competition, cut most of its workforce, and dramatically restructured. (Source: “But by the late 1980s”)
11. The 1992 account describes historic buildings housing new enterprises, old machinery being used in exhibits, and a rising population after decades of decline. It also reports new immigrants from Cambodia, Laos, Latin America, and elsewhere, alongside park tours, exhibits, festivals, concerts, demonstrations, and the Canalway pedestrian path. These are the account’s descriptions, not current measurements. (Source: final paragraphs; publication cited as Dublin, 1992)
12. The source characterizes boom and bust, and technological innovation and obsolescence, as recurring Lowell themes. It attributes to “many observers” the view that technology, improved education, and cultural vitality had positioned the city well for transition to internationally interdependent economies. (Source: “Boom and bust”)
### Interpretations
1. The source supports a shift from industrial decline toward heritage-based redevelopment and new uses for buildings, but presents that shift alongside other revival factors and a later downturn. (Facts 1–3, 7–10)
### Unknowns
- The account does not isolate or quantify how much the parks, university, Wang, building rehabilitation, or other factors contributed to employment or economic recovery; it presents them as concurrent contributors, not proof of a single cause.
- It gives no basis for assessing whether the late-1980s downturn was temporary or whether the reported recovery endured after the 1992 publication. The reported rising population and revitalization should not be treated as present-day conditions.
- The source reports the view of “many observers” that Lowell was positioned for future transition, but does not establish that this anticipated outcome occurred.
### Development sequence
The source documents declining textile employment and early mill departures (from 1890s–1918), further closures (1926 onward), Depression-era hardship, a temporary wartime rise followed by postwar decline, and citizen-led planning in the 1960s. The city endorsed the park idea in 1972; Heritage State Park emerged in 1974, the university formed in 1975, and the National Historical Park and Preservation Commission followed in 1978. The account then reports late-1970s/early-1980s expansion and building reuse, followed by regional cooling and Wang’s cuts and restructuring by the late 1980s. (Facts 1–10)
### Editorial relevance
Facts 1–6 establish industrial decline and human consequences; Facts 7–9 cover civic collaboration, parks, other revival contributions, and building reuse; Facts 10–12 preserve the downturn, the source’s 1992 perspective, and its qualified future-facing interpretation.

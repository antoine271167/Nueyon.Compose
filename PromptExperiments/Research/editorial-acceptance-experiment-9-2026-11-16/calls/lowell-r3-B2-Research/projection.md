### Facts

1. The source is Thomas Dublin’s 1992 National Park Service guide to Lowell National Historical Park and Lowell Heritage State Park; its account is a historical perspective, not a current city measurement. (Source citation: “Dublin, Thomas. 1992.”)
2. Lowell’s textile employment declined from 17,000 in 1895 to fewer than 14,000 in 1918, despite rising output enabled by technological advances. By 1936, textile employment had fallen to 8,000. (Passages beginning “There were early signs” and “The Depression came early”)
3. The source says mill owners knew in the 1890s that their mills were aging and increasingly uncompetitive, but chose not to modernize Lowell operations; some relocated or invested profits in modern Southern plants. It describes Southern promoters as emphasizing lower costs and other advantages, including company towns without union influences and restrictive worker health and safety laws. (Passages beginning “Lowell mill owners knew” and “Southern community and business leaders”)
4. Closures and departures began before the Depression: Bigelow departed in 1914 and Middlesex Mills stopped production in 1918. A wave of closures followed in 1926; Appleton moved production South and Saco-Lowell operations moved to Maine. By the mid-1930s, only the Merrimack, Lawrence, and Boott remained of Lowell’s first large mills. (Passage beginning “As early as World War I”)
5. The source describes severe human effects during the Depression: families faced unemployment and food hardship; children left school for work; jobs were scarce and irregular, and employers made demands on workers. A former worker recalled being sent home repeatedly when there was no work. (Passage beginning “Families coped as best they could”)
6. In the late 1930s, mills increased production and used “stretch-out”—more machines per worker for the same wage. Workers sought better wages and conditions; at Merrimack, women played a significant role in organizing a union in 1938 and went on strike after demands were rejected. The owners capitulated after seven weeks amid approaching war and federal pressure on mills for cloth. (Passages beginning “As the few remaining large mills” and “When a union was formed”)
7. World War II brought a temporary employment and wage boom: remaining mills increased employment, while military-contract firms competed for workers; the source gives weekly wage figures rising from a typical mill starting wage of $13 in 1938 to $29 in 1943, and an average of $37 at Remington in 1943 for a 48-hour week. After the war ended in 1945, textile and munitions orders fell; the source says Lowell returned to economic doldrums, with Boott and Merrimack closing in the 1950s. (Passages beginning “World War II quickened Lowell’s economy” and “The boom proved only temporary”)
8. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens devised a revitalization strategy based on the city’s architectural and cultural heritage, working with urban planners and historians; a proposed historical park would present the city as a “living museum.” The city council endorsed the idea in 1972. Lowell Heritage State Park emerged in 1974, Lowell National Historical Park in 1978, and the Lowell Historic Preservation Commission was created in 1978 to assist park development, promote preservation of downtown buildings and canals, and develop related cultural programs. The source describes cooperation among political and business leaders and multiple groups and government agencies. (Passage beginning “In the 1960s a group of Lowell citizens”)
9. The source identifies additional contributors to the city’s revival: the University of Lowell formed in 1975 through the union of Lowell Technological Institute and Lowell State College, with a mission that included regional economic development; Wang Laboratories arrived in the mid-1970s; and Massachusetts experienced broader prosperity in the late 1970s and early 1980s. (Passage beginning “Other factors in the 1970s”)
10. During the late-1970s/early-1980s prosperity, employment rose as businesses expanded and “over 100 old buildings were rehabilitated and put to new uses”; the source describes Lowell as a model of historic preservation and urban revival, with visitors returning. It also describes historic buildings later housing new enterprises and old machinery being used in exhibits. (Passages beginning “The late 1970s” and “The citizens of Lowell”)
11. The revival did not continue uninterrupted: by the late 1980s the boom was over, the regional economy had cooled, the computer industry was tightening its belt, and companies were closing or relocating. Wang faced strong competition and cut most of its workforce while restructuring. (Passage beginning “But by the late 1980s”)
12. The source says many observers viewed boom-and-bust cycles as familiar in Lowell and believed its new mix of technology, improved education, and cultural vitality positioned the city for transition; it presents this as their view, not as a demonstrated outcome. It also describes population as rising and the city’s new pride, without providing a measurement date or figures. (Passages beginning “Boom and bust” and “The city’s new pride”)

### Unknowns

- The source does not quantify how much the parks, preservation efforts, the university, Wang, broader regional prosperity, or other factors independently contributed to employment or economic revival; it identifies multiple factors and does not isolate their effects. (Facts 8–10)
- It does not establish that the 1992-described revival continued after the late-1980s downturn, or provide current outcomes or measurements. (Facts 1, 11–12)
- It does not show whether the observers’ expectation of readiness for the coming era was fulfilled. (Fact 12)

### Facts

1. Webb’s fully deployed operational configuration measures approximately 70 feet long, 47 feet wide, and 28 feet tall.
2. The source states that this configuration was too large to fit into any available rocket.
3. The Ariane 5 rocket fairing is approximately 18 feet in diameter and 43 feet tall.
4. Webb was designed to fold compactly to fit inside the Ariane 5 fairing.
5. The source states that Webb’s design was driven by the mission’s science requirements and by the need to fold for launch and deploy in space while in flight to L2.
6. Webb’s primary-mirror wings, secondary mirror, and booms are described as folding components.
7. Each primary-mirror wing holds three mirror segments.
8. Both primary-mirror wings were stowed for launch to fit the mirror into the launch vehicle.
9. Webb’s sunshield has five layers.
10. The source states that each sunshield layer is less than the thickness of a human hair.
11. Webb’s sunshield was folded for deployment in space during flight.
12. The Ariane 5 provided thrust for roughly 26 minutes after liftoff.
13. Moments after the upper-stage engine cut off, Webb separated from the upper stage in its fully stowed state.
14. Deployments began minutes after separation.
15. The solar panel automatically deployed on the first day to supply Webb with power.
16. The high-gain antenna deployed on the first day for high-bandwidth communications.
17. Webb’s remaining deployments were choreographed from ground control by Webb engineers.
18. Deployment progress was monitored via telemetry data.
19. The deployment process included over 50 major deployments.
20. The deployment process included 178 release mechanisms that had to work properly.
21. The source states there were over 300 possible single points of failure.
22. The source states that a repair mission was not possible because Webb operates approximately one million miles from Earth.
23. The Ariane launch sent Webb on a direct route to L2 without first orbiting Earth.
24. A trajectory-correction maneuver was executed during the first day after launch.
25. The source states that Ariane’s launch was so accurate that very little correction was needed.
26. In the first week, the fore and aft sunshield pallets deployed.
27. The spacecraft bus and telescope were separated by extending a telescoping tower.
28. The tower extended about two meters.
29. The source states that the tower extension was necessary at that point in the sequence for the rest of the sunshield deployment to proceed.
30. The sunshield membranes were unpinned, and the telescoping midbooms were extended, first on the port side and then on the starboard side.
31. The last sunshield deployment step was tensioning the membranes.
32. Radiators were released and deployed during the sunshield deployment period.
33. During the second week after launch, the secondary-mirror tripod was unfolded and latched.
34. During the second week after launch, the two primary-mirror wings were rotated and latched.
35. The source states that these steps brought Webb into its fully deployed operational configuration.
36. The telescope and scientific instruments started cooling in the shade of the sunshield.
37. The source states that cooling to stable temperatures took several weeks.
38. Cooldown was controlled with strategically placed electric heater strips.
39. The source states that the heater strips were used so components would shrink carefully and water trapped inside the observatory could escape as gas rather than freeze as ice on mirrors or detectors.
40. During the first month, the primary-mirror segments and secondary mirror were unlocked, and their movement was verified.
41. Near the end of the first month, a mid-course maneuver was executed to insert Webb into its optimum orbit around L2.
42. Scientific instrument systems were powered up during the first month.
43. The remaining five months of commissioning involved aligning the optics and calibrating the scientific instruments.
44. During the second, third, and fourth months, Webb’s optics underwent initial checkouts and telescope alignment.
45. Webb was pointed at a single bright star to demonstrate that it could acquire and lock onto targets.
46. Before the primary-mirror segments were aligned to work as a single mirror, images of the same target star were distorted.
47. The alignment process began by identifying which primary-mirror segment corresponded to each image, moving the segments one at a time.
48. The alignment process ended with the primary-mirror segments aligned as one and the secondary mirror aligned optimally.
49. In the fifth and sixth months, the scientific instruments’ modes of operation were calibrated while representative targets were observed.
50. Webb demonstrated the ability to track moving targets, including nearby asteroids, comets, moons, and planets in the solar system.
51. Early Release Observations were made for release after commissioning ended.
52. After six months, Webb began its science mission and routine science operations.
53. The source describes Webb’s deployment as completed with remarkable smoothness.
54. The source states that Webb continued its cooldown phase while meeting thresholds for mirror alignment and later for instruments to come online and be calibrated.
55. The source states that the final results exceeded requirements and expectations.

### Unknowns

- The source does not state the decision criteria used to select specific design or deployment options beyond the stated need to fold for launch and deploy in space, and the mission’s science requirements.
- The source does not identify which, if any, of the possible single points of failure occurred during deployment.
- The source does not give a more detailed account of what made the deployment choreography or ground control necessary beyond the described deployment sequence and monitoring.
- The source does not establish that completing deployment alone meant Webb was ready for routine science operations; it describes additional cooldown, alignment, calibration, and commissioning steps before routine science operations began after six months.
- The source does not state a general lesson about complex projects or describe the deployment as a test of patience.

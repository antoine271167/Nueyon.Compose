### Facts
1. The OPG says fees for POA applications must cover processing costs; fees received exceeded processing costs between 2013 and 2017, prompting a new service to let users apply for refunds. (“Why the service was needed so quickly”)
2. The team built a digital and assisted-digital service with no paper version, including a case-management application to process and approve or reject claims. (Opening; “Why the service was needed so quickly”)
3. The service passed alpha and beta Service Standard assessments and launched to the public in 12 weeks. (Opening)
4. The team said the initial request was to deliver in a matter of weeks, but it quickly judged that timeframe incompatible with creating a quality service. Other stated challenges included inaccurate user records that prevented direct refunds, a team distributed across London, Birmingham and Nottingham, a ministerial reshuffle, and sensitive research with a limited group of POA applicants from 2013–2017. (“The challenges we faced”)
5. The team focused first on people applying for refunds and took that work to alpha; plans for caseworkers came later. The author says the caseworker user did not yet exist before applications came through, and that limited time made avoiding unsupported assumptions important. (“Know your users”)
6. During private beta, the team built the case-management application while iterating the front end. It split fortnightly sprints: the first week for testing, research and accepting applications; the second for suspending submissions and focusing on testing, research and processing applications. (“Plan your research”)
7. The author recommends designing end-to-end services rather than “form fillers,” saying they help internal and external users achieve goals more efficiently. (“Build services not form fillers”)
8. After five weeks of public availability, the author reported over 100,000 applications, 92.6% user satisfaction and an 80.2% completion rate. The source does not provide the measurement methods or comparison baseline. (“5 weeks and going strong…”)
9. The author reports that GDS used the service as a best-practice example in Service Standards training and quotes its assessment report describing the team’s approach as “exemplary.” This is an attributed assessment, not independent evidence of the effect of any individual practice. (“5 weeks and going strong…”)

### Interpretations
- Facts 5–6 show that the team sequenced work around the applicant journey and organized research, submissions and casework in a repeating sprint pattern.

### Unknowns
- Although the source identifies the service as assisted digital, it does not explain what assistance was provided, to whom, or how users accessed it; it also does not report uptake or outcomes for assisted-digital users.
- The source reports the 12-week launch and five-week results, but does not establish that the user ordering, sprint structure, or any other single practice caused the speed, satisfaction or completion results.
- The source gives no measurement definitions, sampling details, or comparison data for satisfaction and completion, and does not break out outcomes by user type or channel. The reported figures therefore do not establish results for assisted-digital users specifically.

### Development sequence
- The source says a team was set up late the previous year to build the refund service and case-management application (Fact 1). The team focused first on refund applicants and took that work to alpha; caseworker plans came later (Fact 5). During private beta, it built case management while iterating the front end and used the described two-week sprint pattern (Fact 6). The service passed alpha and beta assessments and launched in 12 weeks (Fact 3). After five weeks of availability, the author reported the figures in Fact 8.

### Editorial relevance
- Relevant evidence: Facts 1–8; attributed external evaluation: Fact 9. Limits on assisted-digital detail and causal or measurement claims: Unknowns.

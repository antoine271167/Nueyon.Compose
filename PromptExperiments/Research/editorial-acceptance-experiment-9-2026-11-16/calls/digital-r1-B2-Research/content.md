### Facts
1. OPG says it cannot make a profit from POA application fees: annual fee income must equal processing costs. Fees were set each year using predictions, and income exceeded processing costs between 2013 and 2017. (“Why the service was needed so quickly”)
2. A team was set up to create a new service for applying for refunds, alongside a case-management application for processing and approving or rejecting claims. The service was digital and assisted digital, with no paper version. (“Late last year…”; opening description)
3. OPG said people often did not update their details, so it could not refund applicants directly. (“Our data is not always accurate”)
4. The team was initially asked to deliver in weeks, but quickly concluded that this was not possible while creating a quality service for users. The source says the service launched to the public in 12 weeks and that the team passed alpha and beta Service Standard assessments. (Opening description; “We had a tight deadline”)
5. Other stated constraints were a team working across London, Birmingham and Nottingham; a ministerial reshuffle; and sensitive research with a limited, specific user group—POA applicants from 2013–2017. (“The challenges we faced”)
6. The team focused first on people applying for refunds and took that work to alpha; plans for caseworkers came later. The author’s stated rationale was that, until applications came through, the caseworker user did not yet exist in this new service, and the limited time made avoiding unsupported assumptions important. (“Know your users”)
7. During private beta, the team built the case-management application while iterating the front end. It split fortnightly sprints: week one for testing, research and accepting applications; week two suspended submissions to focus on testing, research and processing applications. The author says this helped the team build quickly while ensuring the service met user needs. (“Plan your research”)
8. The author reports that after five weeks of public availability the service had received over 100,000 applications, with 92.6% user satisfaction and an 80.2% completion rate. These are reported results; the source gives no metric definitions or comparison. (“5 weeks and going strong…”)
9. The author says GDS used the service in Service Standards training as an example of best practice and quotes its assessment report: “the service team are developing the service using an exemplary agile service design approach.” (“5 weeks and going strong…”)

### Interpretations
1. The reported delivery approach combined prioritising refund applicants before caseworkers with a recurring split between accepting applications and processing them. (Facts 6–7)

### Unknowns
- The source confirms an assisted-digital service, but does not describe what assistance was provided, who received it, or how it supported people unable to complete the process independently. (Fact 2)
- It does not explain how refunds were ultimately paid despite the stated problem with outdated applicant details, or how the new service handled that constraint. (Fact 3)
- The source reports the 12-week launch and the five-week results, but does not establish that any particular practice caused the launch timing or the application, satisfaction or completion figures. It supplies no comparison, metric definitions, or measurement method. (Facts 4, 7–8)
- The source does not specify the exact start point used for the 12-week delivery period. (Fact 4)

### Development sequence
The team was set up to build the service (Fact 2). It prioritised refund applicants for alpha, with caseworker plans coming later (Fact 6). During private beta, it built case management alongside front-end iteration and used the described fortnightly sprint pattern (Fact 7). The source reports a public launch in 12 weeks, passage of alpha and beta assessments, and results after five weeks of availability; it does not give further precise dates for these milestones (Facts 4, 8).

### Editorial relevance
Facts 1–4 establish the refund rationale, service form, constraints and reported delivery period. Facts 6–7 support the user-ordering and research/operations approach. Facts 8–9 provide attributed early results and assessment evidence; Unknowns qualify assisted-digital detail and causal claims.

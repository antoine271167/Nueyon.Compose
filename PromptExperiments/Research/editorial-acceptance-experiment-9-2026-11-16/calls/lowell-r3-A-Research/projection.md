### Facts

1. After 1890, total employment in Lowell’s textile industry declined from 17,000 in 1895 to fewer than 14,000 in 1918.
2. Lowell mill owners knew by the 1890s that their mills were aging and becoming increasingly noncompetitive.
3. Mill management chose not to modernize its Lowell operations.
4. Mill owners either took operations elsewhere or used profits from Lowell mills to finance modern textile plants in the South.
5. The source says Lowell firms began to fail or leave town as early as World War I.
6. The Bigelow Carpet Company left Lowell in 1914.
7. Middlesex Mills ceased production in 1918.
8. A wave of mill closings came in 1926.
9. The Appleton Company moved production to the South.
10. Operations at the Saco-Lowell Shop shifted north to Maine.
11. By the mid-1930s, only the Merrimack, Lawrence, and Boott remained in operation among Lowell’s first large mills.
12. By 1936, Lowell’s total textile employment had fallen to 8,000.
13. During the Depression, many Lowell families experienced unemployment.
14. A Polish-born worker described her family eating dandelion greens in summer and hard bread in winter, and occasionally selling possessions to buy meat.
15. The source says children quit school and took whatever work they could find.
16. One former mill worker recalled being sent home repeatedly when there was no work, without being told whether work would be available the next day.
17. In the late 1930s, the remaining large mills increased production.
18. The mills’ “stretch-out” practice assigned more machines to workers for the same wage.
19. Workers wanted paid vacations and improved working conditions.
20. Women played a significant role in organizing workers when a union formed to bargain with the Merrimack Mills in 1938.
21. After the workers’ demands for better wages and working conditions were rejected, they went on strike.
22. The Merrimack Mills’ owners capitulated after seven weeks, and the workers returned to the mills.
23. During World War II, the remaining Lowell textile mills increased employment dramatically.
24. A typical starting wage in the mills rose from $13 a week in 1938 to $29 by 1943.
25. When the war ended in 1945, orders for munitions and textiles fell off.
26. The Boott and Merrimack mills closed in the 1950s.
27. The Merrimack mills and boardinghouses were later affected by urban renewal programs in the 1960s.
28. In the 1960s, a group of Lowell citizens devised a strategy to revitalize the community, transform the educational system, and stimulate the local economy.
29. The citizens worked with urban planners and historians on a redevelopment plan based on Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage.
30. One proposal was for a historical park that would present the city as a living museum.
31. Lowell’s city council endorsed the idea in 1972.
32. Lowell Heritage State Park emerged in 1974.
33. Lowell National Historical Park was established in 1978.
34. The Lowell Historic Preservation Commission was created in 1978 to assist the park’s development, stimulate historic preservation of downtown buildings and canals, and develop cultural programs related to the park’s themes.
35. The source describes the park as bringing together the energies, funds, and talents of many groups and government agencies toward common goals.
36. The University of Lowell emerged from the union of Lowell Technological Institute and Lowell State College in 1975.
37. The University of Lowell’s mission included support for regional economic development.
38. Wang Laboratories arrived in Lowell in the mid-1970s.
39. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, employment in Lowell rose as business expanded.
40. Over 100 old buildings were rehabilitated and put to new uses during that period.
41. The source says visitors again came to Lowell, which it describes as a model of historic preservation and urban revival.
42. By the late 1980s, the boom was over and the region’s economy had cooled.
43. Wang faced strong competition and cut most of its workforce while dramatically restructuring its operations.
44. The source says historic buildings house new enterprises.
45. The source identifies its account as a 1992 guide to Lowell National Historical Park and Lowell Heritage State Park.

### Unknowns

- The source does not establish that the decline of Lowell’s textile industry caused the citizens’ heritage-based redevelopment plan, or that the plan was chosen specifically in response to mill decline.
- The source does not establish how much the historical parks or historic preservation contributed to the employment rise, business expansion, or reuse of buildings.
- The source does not establish the effects of the parks or redevelopment on residents’ employment, income, or living conditions.
- The source does not explain why the citizens chose architectural and cultural heritage as the basis for redevelopment beyond describing the plan and its stated aims.
- The source does not establish whether Lowell’s economic recovery continued after the late-1980s downturn. Its account is from 1992 and provides no later measurement.
- The source does not establish that the city “escaped” change; it describes both redevelopment and a later regional economic downturn, but does not assess the city’s overall condition after that downturn.
- The source does not quantify the scale or economic effects of the “new enterprises” housed in historic buildings.

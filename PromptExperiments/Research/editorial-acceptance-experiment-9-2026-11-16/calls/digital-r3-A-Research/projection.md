### Facts

1. The author is the Product Manager for the Power of Attorney (POA) refunds service at the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG).
2. The author says their team built a digital and assisted-digital refunds service.
3. The service had no paper version.
4. The service allowed users to apply for a refund.
5. The service included a case-management application for processing claims.
6. The case-management application was used to approve or reject claims.
7. The OPG cannot make a profit from fees paid for POA applications.
8. The amount received in fees each year must equal the cost of processing applications.
9. Fees are adjusted annually based on predictions of submitted applications.
10. Between 2013 and 2017, the amount received in fees exceeded processing costs.
11. A team was set up late the previous year to build the refunds service.
12. The service passed alpha and beta Service Standard assessments.
13. The service was launched to the public in 12 weeks.
14. The source says OPG data is not always accurate.
15. The source says people tend not to update their details with OPG.
16. The source states that this meant applicants could not be refunded directly.
17. The team was initially asked to deliver the service in a matter of weeks.
18. The author says the team quickly discovered that delivery in that timeframe would not be possible if they wanted to create a quality service for users.
19. The team worked across three sites: London, Birmingham and Nottingham.
20. The ministers in charge were changing during a ministerial reshuffle.
21. The author describes the project as sensitive.
22. The author says the team had to be sensitive in its approach to user research.
23. The user group for research was limited and specific: POA applicants from 2013 to 2017.
24. The author says this made the research trickier.
25. The team focused first on users applying for refunds.
26. The team took that focus to the alpha assessment.
27. Plans for the caseworker user came later.
28. The author says the team chose this order because the caseworker user did not yet exist until applications were coming through, as the service was completely new.
29. The author says that, given the limited time, the team wanted to avoid straying off course because of a lack of relevant knowledge or incorrect assumptions.
30. During private beta, the team began building the case-management application while iterating the front end.
31. The team split fortnightly sprints into two parts.
32. In the first week, the team tested, researched and accepted applications.
33. In the second week, the team suspended submissions to test, research and process applications.
34. The author says this sprint arrangement was key to building the service quickly while ensuring it met user needs.
35. The author recommends being pragmatic and realistic with stakeholders about what can be achieved and in what timeframe.
36. The author recommends presenting options to stakeholders, including their pros and cons.
37. The team set a mission statement with measurable key performance indicators (KPIs).
38. The author says the mission statement and KPIs helped support the plan and provide evidence for decisions with stakeholders and in assessments.
39. The author says user research and evidence were key for each challenge faced.
40. The author advises building services rather than form fillers.
41. The author states that end-to-end services help internal and external users achieve their goals more efficiently.
42. At the time of writing, the service had been available for five weeks.
43. At that time, the service had received over 100,000 applications.
44. The source reports user satisfaction of 92.6% at that time.
45. The source reports a completion rate of 80.2% at that time.
46. The source says the Government Digital Service (GDS) was using the team as an example of best practice in its Service Standards training.
47. The source quotes the assessment report describing the team as developing the service using “an exemplary agile service design approach.”

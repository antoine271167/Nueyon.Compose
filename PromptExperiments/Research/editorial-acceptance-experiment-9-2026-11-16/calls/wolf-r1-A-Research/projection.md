### Facts

1. By the early 1900s, wolves had disappeared throughout most of the United States; the source attributes this to habitat loss and extermination programs.
2. In 1973, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the northern Rocky Mountain wolf as endangered.
3. In 1973, the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem was designated as one of three wolf recovery areas.
4. From 1995 to 1997, 41 wild wolves from Canada and northwest Montana were released in Yellowstone.
5. Wolves from the growing population dispersed to establish territories outside the park.
6. The source states that these wolves are less protected from human-caused mortalities outside the park.
7. The park has provided a place for research on how wolves may affect many aspects of the ecosystem.
8. Wolves live in packs and are highly social animals.
9. In Yellowstone, average pack size is 11.8 individuals.
10. The source describes a pack as a social family with older members, often the alpha male and alpha female, and subordinate members.
11. The source states that pack members have individual personality traits and roles.
12. Packs defend territory from other packs by howling and scent-marking with urine.
13. Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted cooperative care of offspring among wolves.
14. Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted group hunting of large prey among wolves.
15. Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted defense of territory and prey carcasses among wolves.
16. Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted survival benefits to infirmed individuals from social living.
17. In Yellowstone, elk make up 90% of wolves’ winter prey.
18. Deer make up 10–15% of wolves’ summer prey in Yellowstone.
19. Wolves also kill bison.
20. When wolves kill an elk, ravens and magpies arrive almost immediately.
21. Coyotes arrive soon after a wolf kill and wait nearby until the wolves are sated.
22. Bears attempt to chase wolves away from a kill and are usually successful.
23. The source states that many other animals, from eagles to invertebrates, consume the remains of wolf kills.
24. From 1995 to 2000, elk calves made up 50% of wolf prey in early winter.
25. From 1995 to 2000, bull elk made up 25% of wolf prey in early winter.
26. The source says that the early-winter elk calf and bull elk proportions reversed from 2001 to 2007.
27. Elk remains the primary prey of Yellowstone wolves.
28. Bison has become an increasingly important food source for wolves.
29. The source reports some predation on bison of all age classes.
30. The majority of bison consumption by wolves comes from scavenging winter-killed bison or bison that died from injuries sustained during breeding season.
31. The source says changes in wolf predation patterns and impacts on prey species such as elk are linked to other factors, including other predators, management of ungulates outside the park, and weather.
32. The source states that weather patterns influence forage quality and availability and ultimately affect elk nutritional condition.
33. The source states that changes in prey selection and kill rates through time result from complex interactions among these factors.
34. The source says the discovery of changes in prey use emphasizes the importance of long-term monitoring to understand predator-prey dynamics.
35. Current National Park Service research focuses on the relative factors driving wolf predation over the past 25 years.
36. Researchers capture a small proportion of wolves each year and fit them with radio-tracking and GPS collars.
37. The collars enable researchers to gather data on individual wolves.
38. The collars also enable researchers to monitor the population as a whole to see how wolves are affecting other animals and plants within the park.
39. Typically, at the end of each year, 20% of the wolf population is collared.

### Unknowns

- The source does not establish a broad conclusion that wolves, by themselves, govern or control the Yellowstone ecosystem. It says wolves may affect many aspects of the ecosystem and describes particular predator-prey, scavenging, and monitoring evidence.
- The source does not quantify the overall effects of wolves on Yellowstone’s ecosystem or establish the extent of their effects on plants.
- The source does not state that the described pack behaviors, prey relationships, scavenging, and changing prey use together form a single causal network.
- The source does not specify the exact proportions of elk calves and bull elk after the reported reversal from 2001 to 2007.
- The source does not attribute changes in prey selection and kill rates to wolves alone; it identifies complex interactions among multiple factors.
- The source does not establish how much each identified factor contributes to changes in wolf predation.
- The source does not state that prey-use changes caused any particular ecosystem-wide outcome.

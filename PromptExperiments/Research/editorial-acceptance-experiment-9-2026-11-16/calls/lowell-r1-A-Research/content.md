### Facts

1. The source identifies World War I as a short-lived boost to Lowell’s textile and munitions industries, which profited from large military contracts.
2. The source states that Lowell textile employment declined from 17,000 in 1895 to fewer than 14,000 in 1918.
3. The source states that technological advances made output gains possible while mills reduced their workforces.
4. Lowell mill owners knew by the 1890s that their mills were aging and becoming increasingly noncompetitive.
5. Mill management chose not to modernize its Lowell operations.
6. The source says mill owners either moved operations elsewhere or used profits from Lowell mills to finance modern textile plants in the South.
7. The Bigelow Carpet Company left Lowell in 1914.
8. Middlesex Mills ceased production in 1918.
9. In 1926, the Hamilton Company went into receivership, followed by the Suffolk, Tremont, and Massachusetts Mills.
10. The Appleton Company moved production to the South.
11. Operations at the Saco-Lowell Shop shifted north to Maine.
12. By the mid-1930s, only the Merrimack, Lawrence, and Boott remained in operation among Lowell’s first large mills.
13. By 1936, Lowell’s textile employment had fallen to 8,000.
14. A Polish-born worker described her family eating dandelion greens in summer and hard bread in winter during the Depression, and sometimes selling possessions to buy meat.
15. The source states that children left school and took whatever work they could find during the Depression.
16. One former mill worker recalled being sent home when there was no work and receiving no advance notice about whether work would be available the next day.
17. In the late 1930s, the remaining large mills increased production.
18. The source describes the mills’ “stretch-out” as assigning workers more machines for the same wage.
19. Merrimack Mills workers wanted paid vacations; the source says these were denied even to workers who had been with the company for decades.
20. In 1938, a union formed to bargain with Merrimack Mills, and women played a significant role in organizing workers.
21. After their demands for better wages and working conditions were rejected, the workers went on strike.
22. The owners capitulated after seven weeks, and the workers returned to the mills.
23. World War II quickened Lowell’s economy, and the remaining textile mills increased employment dramatically.
24. The source states that wages rose during the wartime period: a typical mill starting wage of $13 a week in 1938 was $29 by 1943.
25. When World War II ended in 1945, orders for munitions and textiles fell, and the city returned to economic doldrums.
26. The Boott and Merrimack mills closed in the 1950s.
27. The source states that the Merrimack’s mills and boardinghouses, along with the Little Canada tenement neighborhood, were affected by urban-renewal programs in the 1960s.
28. Mill employment all but disappeared, and, at that point in the account, nothing had yet taken its place.
29. The source says that for many residents, remembering the past stirred feelings of anger and abandonment.
30. In the 1960s, a group of Lowell citizens devised a strategy to revitalize the community, transform the educational system, and stimulate the local economy.
31. The citizens worked with urban planners and historians on a redevelopment plan based on Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage.
32. One proposal was for a historical park that would present the city as a living museum.
33. Political and business leaders offered support for the movement.
34. The Lowell City Council endorsed the idea in 1972.
35. Lowell Heritage State Park emerged in 1974.
36. Lowell National Historical Park emerged in 1978.
37. The Lowell Historic Preservation Commission was created in 1978 to assist the park’s development, stimulate preservation of downtown buildings and canals, and develop cultural programs related to the park’s themes.
38. The source describes the park as a new kind of park that brought together the energies, funds, and talents of multiple groups and government agencies toward common goals.
39. The University of Lowell emerged in 1975 from the union of Lowell Technological Institute and Lowell State College.
40. The University of Lowell’s mission included support for regional economic development.
41. Wang Laboratories arrived in Lowell in the mid-1970s; the source identifies it then as a leader in computers.
42. The source says many hoped Wang’s arrival would lead to another bright technological future.
43. The source describes the late 1970s and early 1980s as years of prosperity in Massachusetts, with an economy built around higher education, high technology, and cultural amenities.
44. During that period, employment in Lowell rose as business expanded.
45. More than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated and put to new uses during that period.
46. The source says visitors again came to Lowell and describes the city as a model of historic preservation and urban revival.
47. By the late 1980s, the boom was over and the region’s economy had cooled.
48. The source says the computer industry was tightening its belt and many companies were closing or relocating.
49. Wang faced strong competition, which the source says required it to cut most of its workforce and dramatically restructure its operations.
50. The source states that historic buildings housed new enterprises and old machinery was used in new exhibits.
51. The source describes Lowell’s population as rising after decades of decline.
52. The source identifies the book as published in 1992.

### Development sequence

- The source describes a short-lived World War I boost to Lowell’s textile and munitions industries (Fact 1), alongside declining textile employment (Fact 2).
- Mill owners chose not to modernize Lowell operations (Fact 5); some operations left or profits went toward plants in the South (Fact 6). The source does not establish a further relationship between these statements and each subsequent closure beyond the specific details it gives.
- Companies closed, left, or moved operations from Lowell in the 1910s and 1920s (Facts 7–11); by the mid-1930s, only three of the first large mills remained (Fact 12).
- Textile employment had fallen to 8,000 by 1936 (Fact 13). The source also describes Depression-era hardship and workers’ experiences (Facts 14–16).
- The remaining large mills increased production in the late 1930s (Fact 17); the source describes the stretch-out and workers’ demands (Facts 18–19). In 1938, a union formed, workers struck, and they returned after the owners capitulated seven weeks later (Facts 20–22).
- World War II quickened the economy and remaining mills increased employment (Fact 23); the source records rising mill wages (Fact 24).
- After the war ended in 1945, orders fell and Lowell returned to economic doldrums (Fact 25). The source later records the closure of the Boott and Merrimack mills in the 1950s (Fact 26).
- In the 1960s, urban-renewal programs affected mills, boardinghouses, and Little Canada (Fact 27); mill employment had nearly disappeared and nothing had yet taken its place (Fact 28).
- In the 1960s, citizens devised a redevelopment strategy and worked with planners and historians on a heritage-based plan (Facts 30–31); a historical park was among the proposals (Fact 32).
- The City Council endorsed the idea in 1972 (Fact 34). Lowell Heritage State Park emerged in 1974 (Fact 35); Lowell National Historical Park and the Lowell Historic Preservation Commission emerged in 1978 (Facts 36–37).
- The University of Lowell was formed in 1975 (Fact 39). Wang Laboratories arrived in the mid-1970s (Fact 41). The source does not establish a causal relationship among these events or between them and the park initiatives.
- The source describes Massachusetts prosperity in the late 1970s and early 1980s (Fact 43), when Lowell employment rose and more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated and reused (Facts 44–45). The source does not establish which developments caused these outcomes.
- By the late 1980s, the boom was over and the regional economy had cooled (Fact 47); the source also describes changes in the computer industry and companies closing or relocating (Fact 48), and Wang’s workforce cuts and restructuring (Fact 49).
- In the account published in 1992, historic buildings housed new enterprises and old machinery was used in exhibits (Fact 50). The source does not establish the later course of these activities.

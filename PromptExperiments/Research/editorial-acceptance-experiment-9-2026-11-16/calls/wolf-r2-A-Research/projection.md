### Facts

- **Fact 1.** The source says wolves once ranged from the Arctic tundra to Mexico.
- **Fact 2.** Habitat loss and extermination programs led to wolves’ demise throughout most of the United States by the early 1900s.
- **Fact 3.** In 1973, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the northern Rocky Mountain wolf as endangered and designated the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem as one of three recovery areas.
- **Fact 4.** From 1995 to 1997, 41 wild wolves from Canada and northwest Montana were released in Yellowstone.
- **Fact 5.** Wolves from the growing population dispersed to establish territories outside the park.
- **Fact 6.** The park has provided a place for research on how wolves may affect many aspects of the ecosystem.
- **Fact 7.** Yellowstone wolves live in packs; their average pack size is 11.8 individuals.
- **Fact 8.** The source describes a pack as a social family whose members have individual personality traits and roles.
- **Fact 9.** Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted social living’s adaptive value for cooperative care of offspring.
- **Fact 10.** Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted social living’s adaptive value for group hunting of large prey.
- **Fact 11.** Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted social living’s adaptive value for defense of territory and prey carcasses.
- **Fact 12.** Yellowstone research since reintroduction has highlighted survival benefits of social living for infirmed individuals.
- **Fact 13.** In Yellowstone, 90% of wolves’ winter prey is elk.
- **Fact 14.** Deer make up 10–15% of wolves’ summer prey in Yellowstone.
- **Fact 15.** Wolves also kill bison.
- **Fact 16.** The source states that many other animals benefit from wolf kills.
- **Fact 17.** Ravens and magpies arrive almost immediately after wolves kill an elk.
- **Fact 18.** Coyotes arrive soon after an elk kill and wait nearby until the wolves are sated.
- **Fact 19.** Bears attempt to chase wolves away from an elk kill and are usually successful.
- **Fact 20.** The source says animals ranging from eagles to invertebrates consume the remains of wolf kills.
- **Fact 21.** From 1995 to 2000, in early winter, elk calves comprised 50% of wolf prey and bull elk comprised 25%.
- **Fact 22.** The source says that ratio reversed from 2001 to 2007.
- **Fact 23.** Although elk remains the primary prey, bison has become an increasingly important food source for wolves.
- **Fact 24.** Most bison consumption by wolves comes from scavenging winter-killed bison or bison dying from breeding-season injuries.
- **Fact 25.** The source says changes in wolf predation patterns and impacts on prey species such as elk are inextricably linked to other factors, including other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather.
- **Fact 26.** The source says weather patterns influence forage quality and availability, which affect elk nutritional condition.
- **Fact 27.** The source says changes in prey selection and kill rates through time result from complex interactions among these factors.
- **Fact 28.** The source says the discovery of changes in prey composition emphasizes the importance of long-term monitoring to understand predator-prey dynamics.
- **Fact 29.** Current National Park Service research focuses on the relative factors driving wolf predation over the past 25 years.
- **Fact 30.** Each year, park researchers capture a small proportion of wolves and fit them with radio tracking and GPS collars.
- **Fact 31.** The collars enable researchers to gather data on individual wolves and monitor the population as a whole to see how wolves are affecting other animals and plants within the park.
- **Fact 32.** Typically, only 20% of the wolf population is collared at the end of each year.
- **Fact 33.** The park’s wolf population has stabilized since 2009, after declines from the initial recolonization.
- **Fact 34.** Most of the population decrease has been in packs on the northern range; the source attributes it primarily to the decline in the elk population and available territory.
- **Fact 35.** Canine distemper and sarcoptic mange have also been factors in the population decline.

### Unknowns

- The source does not establish that wolves alone control or govern the Yellowstone ecosystem.
- The source does not quantify the overall effects of wolves on the ecosystem or establish the direction or magnitude of effects across its many aspects.
- The source says collars are used to monitor how wolves are affecting other animals and plants, but does not provide specific findings about effects on plants.
- The source does not specify the relative contribution of each listed factor to changes in wolf predation patterns or elk impacts; it says current research focuses on the relative factors driving wolf predation.
- The source does not establish that the changing prey composition or the other listed factors caused a particular ecosystem-wide outcome.
- The source does not establish that the listed food relationships and pack behaviors amount to a complete account of Yellowstone’s ecosystem relationships.

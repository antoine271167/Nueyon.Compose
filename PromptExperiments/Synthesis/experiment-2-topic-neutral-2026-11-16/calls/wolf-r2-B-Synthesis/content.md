### Central insight
In Yellowstone, the wolf is not established as a standalone ecosystem controller: its role is described through social behavior and food relationships, while prey selection and predation vary amid several interacting conditions.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone packs are described as social families; their behaviors include cooperative offspring care and group hunting of large prey.
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals, including ravens, magpies, coyotes, bears, eagles, and invertebrates.
3. The prey profile changed over time: in early winter, elk calves made up 50% of wolf prey and bull elk 25% in 1995–2000; those proportions reversed in 2001–2007.
4. The source links changes in wolf predation and elk impacts to interacting factors, including other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather. It does not quantify each factor’s contribution.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the focus on how these documented relationships complicate a simple one-predator-controls-the-ecosystem picture. The changing prey profile and the source’s account of interacting factors are the central evidence; pack behavior and scavengers can provide brief examples of social and food relationships. Explain what Yellowstone research describes, rather than turning it into advice or a universal rule for readers.

### What should be de-emphasized
The wolves’ reintroduction history, population trends, disease outbreaks, and pack-versus-pack mortality are not needed to establish this story. Avoid a broad account of wolf management or Yellowstone’s full ecology.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not establish a measured, ecosystem-wide cascade or show that wolves control the ecosystem as a whole. It also does not quantify the relative contribution of the factors associated with changes in predation and elk impacts. Do not present the described relationships as proof of a single cause or a general ecological rule.

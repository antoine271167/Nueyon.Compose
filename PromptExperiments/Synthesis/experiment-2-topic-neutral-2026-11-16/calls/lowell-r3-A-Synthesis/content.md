### Central insight
Lowell’s heritage-based redevelopment accompanied a period of building reuse and rising activity in the late 1970s and early 1980s, but the boom had ended by the late 1980s, complicating a simple story of lasting recovery.

### Evidence
1. Textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936; by then only three of Lowell’s first large mills remained. Workers’ recollections in the account also describe scarce work and uncertainty about whether jobs would be available.
2. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens developed a revitalization strategy centered partly on the city’s architectural and cultural heritage. The city council endorsed the historical-park idea in 1972; state and national parks followed in 1974 and 1978. The account describes cooperation among citizen groups, political and business leaders, and government agencies.
3. The account reports rising employment and business expansion in the late 1970s and early 1980s, alongside the rehabilitation and new use of more than 100 old buildings. It does not attribute those developments solely to the parks.
4. By the late 1980s, the account says the boom had ended as the regional economy cooled and companies closed or relocated.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the story focused on the contrast between textile decline, heritage-based redevelopment and building reuse, and the later end of the boom. The worker recollections can make the earlier decline tangible, but need not become a separate story about labor conditions. Present the redevelopment as Lowell’s historical experience, not as advice or a universal formula for cities.

### What should be de-emphasized
The detailed chronology of mill closures, the strike, and wartime employment are not needed to establish this story. The University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories are relevant to the wider account of 1970s change, but their histories need not be covered here.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not quantify how much the parks, preservation programs, university, Wang, or other factors contributed to recovery; avoid implying that the parks caused the reported economic changes. The evidence describes the account’s perspective in 1992 and does not establish what happened afterward or describe Lowell’s current conditions.

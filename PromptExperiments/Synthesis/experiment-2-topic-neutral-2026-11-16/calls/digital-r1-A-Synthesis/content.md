### Central insight
The POA refund service launched publicly in 12 weeks; the account describes a delivery sequence that prioritized refund applicants before caseworkers and a fortnightly rhythm that made time for research and testing, but does not establish that these choices caused the launch timing.

### Evidence
1. The service launched to the public in 12 weeks. The team first focused on refund applicants and took that work to alpha; plans for caseworkers came later.
2. During private beta, the team used fortnightly sprints: one week included research, testing and accepting applications; the next suspended submissions to focus on research, testing and processing applications.
3. The service was digital and assisted-digital, with no paper version. The source does not describe the assistance provided.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Center the documented sequence and research rhythm as choices the POA team made while developing this service. Explain the case as an account of OPG’s delivery approach, not as a universal prescription for service designers. The 12-week launch is useful context, not proof that these practices produced the timeline.

### What should be de-emphasized
Do not turn the story into a full account of the service, its constraints, or its results. The reported application, satisfaction and completion figures after five weeks can be omitted; they do not show that the described practices caused those outcomes. Avoid expanding the assisted-digital detail into a claim about how people needing support were served.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not establish that the sequencing or sprint rhythm caused the 12-week launch or the reported results. It does not explain what assisted-digital support involved. It also does not specify the exact start point for the 12-week period.

### Central insight
Yellowstone’s evidence complicates the idea that one predator governs an ecosystem: wolf prey selection and elk impacts vary amid changing prey conditions and other interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. The share of wolf prey made up by elk calves and bull elk reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007; the source links this change to prey vulnerability and availability.
2. The source describes wolf predation and elk impacts as shaped by interacting factors, including other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the focus on why wolf predation cannot be reduced to a simple, fixed relationship between wolves and elk: prey use changed, and several factors interact. As one optional illustration of food relationships, wolf kills also feed other animals. Explain this as Yellowstone’s documented account, not as advice or a universal rule for readers.

### What should be de-emphasized
Pack cooperation and the details of wolf population history are not needed to establish this story. Avoid turning the account into a broad survey of Yellowstone’s ecosystem or a claim that wolves control it.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not quantify the contribution of each factor to changing predation or elk impacts, and it does not report measured ecosystem-wide outcomes such as a demonstrated cascade. Do not present wolf control of the ecosystem as established.

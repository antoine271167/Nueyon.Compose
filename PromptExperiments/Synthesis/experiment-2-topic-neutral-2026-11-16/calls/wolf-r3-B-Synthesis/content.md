### Central insight
Yellowstone’s wolves are part of changing ecological relationships—not a demonstrated single force controlling the ecosystem: pack cooperation and shared food connect them to other animals, while prey patterns and wolf impacts reflect multiple interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone packs are social families; the source describes cooperative offspring care and group hunting of large prey.
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals: ravens and magpies arrive soon after a kill, and many other species consume the remains.
3. The composition of wolf prey changed over time: during early winter, elk calves made up 50% of wolf prey and bulls 25% in 1995–2000; the proportions reversed in 2001–2007. The source links this shift to changes in prey vulnerability and availability.
4. The source describes changes in wolf predation and elk impacts as involving interacting factors, including other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather. Current research examines the factors’ relative roles.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Center the story on the contrast between a simple predator-controls-ecosystem picture and the relationships and changing conditions documented here. Evidence 1–2 can make the network idea concrete; Evidence 3–4 show that prey patterns and wolf impacts vary amid interacting factors. Explain Yellowstone’s documented case, not a universal rule or advice for readers.

### What should be de-emphasized
Restoration chronology, pack conflicts and mortality, disease outbreaks, and detailed population trends are not needed to establish this story. Avoid turning the scavenger examples into a full species inventory.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not quantify the contribution of each factor to changes in predation or elk impacts, or report measured ecosystem-wide outcomes such as a demonstrated cascade. Do not claim that wolves control the ecosystem, or that their social behaviors alone determine ecological outcomes.

### Central insight
Yellowstone’s wolf story is not one of a solitary predator governing an ecosystem: the source describes wolves acting in social packs, their kills feeding other animals, and predation changing amid interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone packs are social families; the source describes group hunting of large prey and other benefits of social living.
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals, including ravens, magpies, coyotes, bears, eagles, and invertebrates.
3. The share of early-winter wolf prey made up by elk calves and bull elk reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source links changes in prey selection and kill rates to interacting factors, including prey vulnerability and availability, other predators, management outside the park, and weather.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Build the story around how pack behavior and food relationships complicate the image of a wolf as a standalone force, then use the documented prey shift and interacting factors to show why a simple cause-and-effect account is insufficient. Explain Yellowstone’s documented case; do not turn it into advice for readers or a universal rule about ecosystems.

### What should be de-emphasized
The reintroduction timeline, population totals and disease history, territorial conflict details, collaring methods, and the full list of wolf-prey figures are not needed to establish this story. Avoid presenting broad ecosystem effects as demonstrated findings.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not demonstrate ecosystem-wide effects, including measured effects on plants, or quantify the relative contribution of the factors linked to changing predation and elk impacts. Do not claim that wolves control Yellowstone’s ecosystem or that any one factor caused the reported prey shift.

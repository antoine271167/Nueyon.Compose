### Central insight
Yellowstone’s wolves are part of changing ecological relationships—not a single switch that controls the ecosystem: pack behavior, the animals that use wolf kills, and changing prey conditions all shape the picture, while the source does not establish ecosystem-wide control by wolves.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone packs are social families; the source describes cooperative offspring care and group hunting of large prey among the benefits of social living.
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals, including ravens, magpies, coyotes, bears, eagles, and invertebrates.
3. Wolf prey composition changed over time: in early winter, elk calves made up 50% of wolf prey and bull elk 25% in 1995–2000; the proportions reversed in 2001–2007. The source links this change to prey vulnerability and availability.
4. The source also identifies other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather as factors linked to changes in wolf predation and elk impacts, describing the changes as interactions among factors.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the story on the limits of a simple “one predator controls an ecosystem” picture. Use the changing prey composition and interacting conditions as the clearest evidence; pack sociality or the use of wolf kills by other animals can provide one supporting example. Explain what this Yellowstone source describes, not what readers should do or a universal rule about ecosystems.

### What should be de-emphasized
The reintroduction timeline, population trends and diseases, territorial conflict between packs, collaring, and other monitoring details are not needed to establish this story.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not report measured ecosystem-wide effects, such as a demonstrated cascade or specific effects on plants. It also does not quantify the relative contribution of the factors affecting predation and elk impacts. Do not imply that wolves either control the ecosystem as a whole or have no broader effects.

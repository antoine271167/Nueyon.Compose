### Central insight
Yellowstone’s account presents wolves not as solitary ecosystem controllers but as participants in a living network: pack behavior shapes how they hunt, their kills feed other animals, and changes in predation and elk impacts reflect several interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone wolf packs are social families; the source describes cooperative offspring care and group hunting of large prey among the benefits of social living.
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals: ravens and magpies arrive quickly, coyotes wait nearby, bears often chase wolves away from carcasses, and many other species consume the remains.
3. Wolf prey patterns changed: during early winter, elk calves made up half of wolf prey and bull elk a quarter in 1995–2000; those proportions reversed in 2001–2007. The source links changing prey selection and kill rates to interacting factors, including prey availability and vulnerability, other predators, management outside the park, and weather.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the story on how these documented relationships complicate a one-predator-controls-the-ecosystem picture: wolves live and hunt socially, their kills become food for other animals, and prey impacts vary amid multiple interacting conditions. The prey-pattern shift is one useful example, not a reason to claim a single cause. Explain Yellowstone’s documented account, rather than turning it into general advice about ecosystems.

### What should be de-emphasized
The wolf reintroduction timeline, population and disease history, collaring methods, and other details of park monitoring are not needed to establish this story. Avoid expanding into claims about plants or a broader ecosystem cascade.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not establish measured, ecosystem-wide outcomes such as a demonstrated cascade, or quantify how much each interacting factor contributes to changing predation and elk impacts. The reported social behaviors also do not establish that they alone determine pack success or ecosystem outcomes.

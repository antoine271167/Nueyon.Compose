### Central insight
Yellowstone’s evidence complicates the idea of a single predator steering an ecosystem: wolves live and hunt socially, their kills feed other animals, and changes in prey selection and elk impacts occur amid several interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone wolf packs are social families; documented social behaviors include cooperative offspring care and group hunting of large prey.
2. Wolves’ kills provide food for other animals, including ravens, magpies, coyotes, bears, eagles, and invertebrates.
3. The balance of elk calves and bull elk in wolf prey changed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source describes changes in prey selection and kill rates, and their effects on elk, as involving interactions among prey vulnerability and availability, other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the focus on the limits of a simple one-predator-controls-the-ecosystem account. The changing prey pattern and the source’s account of interacting factors provide the clearest evidence for that point; pack behavior and the animals using wolf kills can add a concrete sense of these relationships. Explain the Yellowstone evidence, not a universal lesson or recommendation for readers.

### What should be de-emphasized
The reintroduction timeline, population fluctuations, disease outbreaks, pack territorial conflict, and collaring are part of the broader Yellowstone story but are not needed to establish this insight.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not demonstrate a broad ecosystem-wide cascade or quantify wolves’ effects on plants. It also does not quantify the relative contribution of each factor affecting prey selection and elk impacts. Do not imply that wolf social behavior or predation alone determines ecosystem outcomes.

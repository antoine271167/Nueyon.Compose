### Central insight
Lowell’s turn from textile decline toward heritage-based redevelopment and new activity was not a straight-line recovery: the 1992 account records building reuse and economic growth in the late 1970s and early 1980s, followed by a downturn by the late 1980s, without establishing that preservation efforts caused the earlier gains.

### Evidence
1. Lowell’s textile decline brought substantial job losses: mill employment fell to 8,000 by 1936. Workers’ recollections in the account describe hardship, scarce work, and uncertainty.
2. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens developed a revitalization strategy based on the city’s architectural and cultural heritage. Political and business leaders supported the effort, which included cooperation among groups and government agencies and the creation of heritage and historic parks.
3. The account reports rising employment and business expansion in the late 1970s and early 1980s, alongside more than 100 old buildings rehabilitated for new uses. It does not attribute these developments solely to the parks.
4. By the late 1980s, the account says the boom had ended as the regional and computer economies cooled, companies closed or relocated, and Wang cut most of its workforce and restructured.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the focus on the contrast between Lowell’s civic heritage initiative, the recorded reuse and growth, and the later downturn. The workers’ recollections in Evidence 1 can make the earlier industrial decline concrete if useful. Describe the documented actors’ choices and the account’s historical perspective; do not turn them into advice for readers or claim that the parks produced the recovery.

### What should be de-emphasized
The detailed chronology of individual mill closures and the separate histories of the University of Lowell and Wang’s arrival are not needed to establish this story. Avoid presenting any single institution or initiative as the cause of citywide recovery.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The account does not quantify the separate effects of the parks, preservation programs, university, Wang, or other factors on employment or recovery, and it does not establish whether the revival offset losses for workers affected by closures and layoffs. Treat its descriptions as a 1992 historical perspective, not as measurements of Lowell today.

### Central insight
In the 1992 account, Lowell’s civic effort to make its industrial heritage a basis for renewal coincided with the reuse of old mill buildings and an employment upswing, but a late-1980s downturn prevents the story from being a simple, uninterrupted recovery.

### Evidence
1. Lowell’s textile economy contracted over time: mill employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to under 14,000 in 1918, and by 1936 it was 8,000, with only three of the city’s first large mills remaining.
2. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens proposed revitalization based on the city’s architectural and cultural heritage, including a historical park. The city council endorsed the idea in 1972; heritage and national historical parks followed, alongside a preservation commission. The account describes cooperation among civic groups, political and business leaders, and government agencies.
3. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the account reports rising employment and business expansion, as well as more than 100 old buildings rehabilitated for new uses. It does not attribute these developments solely to the parks or preservation work.
4. By the late 1980s, the account says the boom had ended: the regional economy cooled, the computer industry tightened, and Wang cut most of its workforce and restructured.
5. Optional human context: a worker recalled being sent home repeatedly without knowing whether work would be available, illustrating the uncertainty experienced during the earlier decline.

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the focus on the contrast between Lowell’s documented attempt to use its industrial heritage in renewal and the later downturn. Preserve the distinction between the heritage initiative and the economic developments reported alongside it; the source does not establish that the initiative caused the upswing. The worker recollection in Evidence 5 may add human texture, but is not required. Explain the choices and events described in Lowell’s account, not as advice or a general prescription for other cities.

### What should be de-emphasized
Do not turn this into a full account of every factor in Lowell’s economic change. The University of Lowell and Wang’s arrival are not needed as parallel recovery stories; Wang’s later contraction is relevant only as evidence of the late-1980s downturn. Avoid detailing the full history of mill closures or preservation institutions beyond what establishes the contrast.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The account does not quantify how much the parks, preservation programs, university, Wang, or other factors separately contributed to employment or recovery. It also does not establish whether the later activity offset losses for workers affected by closures and layoffs. This is a historical account from 1992; it provides no basis for describing Lowell’s later or current conditions.

### Central insight
Yellowstone’s wolves are part of an interacting ecological network, not evidence of a simple story in which one predator controls an ecosystem: pack behavior and wolf kills connect them to other animals, while prey use changes amid several interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone wolf packs are social families; the source identifies cooperative care of offspring and group hunting of large prey among the benefits of living in a pack. (Fact 3)
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals, including ravens, magpies, coyotes, bears, eagles and invertebrates. (Fact 6)
3. The share of elk calves and bulls in early-winter wolf prey reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source describes changes in prey selection and kill rates as involving interacting factors, including prey vulnerability and availability, other predators, management outside the park and weather. (Facts 7, 9)

### What the narrative should emphasize
Keep the focus on how wolf behavior, the animals that use wolf kills, and changing prey relationships complicate a single-predator explanation. Explain the documented factors as interacting, without claiming that any one of them caused the prey shift or assigning them relative weight. Describe the source’s account of Yellowstone rather than turning it into advice or a universal rule for readers.

### What should be de-emphasized
The wolf reintroduction and population history, disease outbreaks, territorial conflict, and collaring and monitoring details are not needed to establish this story. Avoid expanding the account into a general overview of wolf recovery or Yellowstone research.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not establish measured, ecosystem-wide effects such as a demonstrated cascade through plants, or quantify the contribution of each factor to changes in predation and elk impacts. Do not claim that wolves control the ecosystem, or that pack behavior alone determines ecological outcomes.

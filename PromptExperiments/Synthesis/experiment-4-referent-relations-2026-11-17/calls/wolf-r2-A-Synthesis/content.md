### Central insight
Yellowstone’s wolves are portrayed not as solitary ecosystem controllers but as social predators whose kills feed other animals, while changes in their prey and impacts occur amid several interacting factors.

### Evidence
1. Yellowstone packs are social families; the source identifies cooperative offspring care and group hunting among their social-living benefits. (Fact 3)
2. Wolf kills provide food for other animals, from ravens and coyotes to bears and invertebrates. (Fact 6)
3. The composition of wolf prey changed over time: the proportion of early-winter prey made up by elk calves and bull elk reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source links such changes to prey vulnerability and availability. (Fact 7)
4. The source describes changes in wolf predation and elk impacts as involving interactions among other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather, and says research is examining these factors’ relative roles. (Fact 9; Fact 13)

### What the narrative should emphasize
Center the contrast between wolves’ social and food relationships and the multiple conditions associated with changing predation. The prey-composition shift in item 3 can serve as one concrete example. Explain the source’s account of Yellowstone, not a general rule or recommendation for readers; do not imply that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes.

### What should be de-emphasized
The reintroduction history, detailed population trends and disease events, and pack territorial conflict are not needed to establish this story. Avoid turning the narrative into a broader account of wolf recovery or monitoring methods.

### Gaps and uncertainty
The source does not quantify the relative contribution of the factors in item 4, nor establish measured plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade. It also does not show that pack social behavior alone determines population or ecosystem outcomes.

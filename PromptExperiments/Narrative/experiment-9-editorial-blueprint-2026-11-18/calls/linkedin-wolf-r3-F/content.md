Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a network of relationships—not just predators pursuing prey.

Wolf packs are social families. They hunt together and cooperate in caring for their young. Their kills also become food for ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.

The prey wolves hunt has changed over time. In early winter, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in wolf prey reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. Changes in prey availability and vulnerability are part of that picture, alongside other predators, elk management outside the park and weather.

Wolves matter to Yellowstone through their relationships with pack members, prey and scavengers. But their changing impacts unfold amid several interacting conditions—not through one predator directing the ecosystem.

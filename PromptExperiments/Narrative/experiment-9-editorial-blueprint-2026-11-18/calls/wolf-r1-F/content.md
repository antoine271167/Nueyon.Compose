**Selected Story** Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a living network, not solitary animals that simply control an ecosystem. Their social lives and kills connect them to other animals, while changes in what they prey on—and in their impacts—occur amid several interacting conditions.

**Story Progression**
- **Context:** Yellowstone wolf packs are social families. Cooperative care of offspring and group hunting are among the social-living benefits described in the source; their kills also provide food for animals ranging from ravens and coyotes to bears and invertebrates.
- **Development:** The prey composition changed over time: the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in early-winter prey reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source links prey changes to vulnerability and availability, and describes wolf predation and elk impacts in connection with other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather.

**Evidence/Observed Results** The shift in the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk is a concrete observed change. The source also reports that wolf kills feed other species. It identifies several interacting factors affecting predation and elk impacts, while research examines their relative roles.

**Editorial Conclusion** Yellowstone’s wolves are best understood here through relationships: pack behavior and kills connect them to other animals, while changes in predation do not have a single cause established by the source.

**Critical Constraints** The source does not quantify the relative contribution of the interacting factors or establish measured plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade. Do not imply that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes.

Lowell’s late-20th-century revival was not a straight line.

Textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936 as mills closed and people left. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens proposed revitalizing the city around its architectural and cultural heritage. Political and business leaders supported the effort, and the city council endorsed it in 1972. Heritage and historical parks followed in 1974 and 1978.

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated for new uses, alongside rising employment and business expansion. The University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories were also developments of the 1970s, part of a broader period of change.

By the late 1980s, a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry—including workforce cuts and restructuring at Wang—had ended the boom.

Reusing Lowell’s industrial heritage was a tangible part of its revival. It was not an escape from the economic changes that continued to shape the city.

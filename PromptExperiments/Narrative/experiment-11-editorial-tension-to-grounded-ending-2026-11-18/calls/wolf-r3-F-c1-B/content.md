In Yellowstone, a wolf hunt can feed animals well beyond the pack.

Wolves live in social groups, hunting together and caring cooperatively for their young. After a kill, ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates can feed on what remains—connecting the pack’s hunt to other animals.

The wolves’ prey has changed, too. Between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in early-winter prey reversed. Changes in prey vulnerability and availability are part of that picture. Wolf predation and its impacts on elk also unfold amid other predators, elk management outside the park and weather.

Yellowstone’s wolves matter through these relationships, but their changing impacts are not the work of one predator alone.

A wolf kill in Yellowstone can feed far more than the pack. Ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates also use the food wolves leave behind.

Wolves themselves live in social family groups. They hunt together and share care of their young—relationships that shape how they live as a pack.

Their relationship with prey changes, too. In early-winter prey between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk reversed. Prey vulnerability and availability are part of that picture, alongside other predators, elk management outside the park and weather.

Yellowstone’s wolves are connected to pack members, prey and scavengers. Their changing impacts are part of a web of interacting conditions, not a story of one predator directing the ecosystem.

Central Message: In Yellowstone, wolves act as social predators whose kills support other animals, while changes in their prey and impacts unfold amid several interacting factors—not wolf control alone.

Story Progression:
- Context: Yellowstone wolf packs are social families that cooperate in caring for offspring and hunting; their kills also feed ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.
- Development: The composition of wolf prey shifted over time: elk calves and bull elk reversed their proportions of early-winter prey between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, reflecting changes linked to prey vulnerability and availability.
- Endpoint: Changes in wolf predation and elk impacts are described alongside other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather. Research is examining these factors’ relative roles, underscoring that wolves are part of a changing network rather than a sole ecosystem controller.

Critical Constraints: Do not claim that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes, that the factors’ relative contributions are known, or that the source establishes plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade.

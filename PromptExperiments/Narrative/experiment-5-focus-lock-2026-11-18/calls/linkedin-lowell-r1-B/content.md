Lowell’s old mills found new uses, but their reuse did not shield the city from economic change.

Textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936 as mills closed. In the 1960s, citizens proposed drawing on Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage as part of the city’s revitalization. Political and business leaders supported the effort, and the city council endorsed it in 1972. Heritage and historical parks followed in 1974 and 1978.

By the late 1970s and early 1980s, more than 100 old buildings had been rehabilitated for new uses. Employment was rising, and businesses were expanding. These developments marked a new chapter for Lowell’s industrial buildings—but not an end to economic cycles.

By the late 1980s, a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry, including workforce cuts and restructuring at Wang Laboratories, accompanied another downturn. Lowell’s reused buildings showed how its industrial past could serve the city in a different way. They did not insulate it from change.

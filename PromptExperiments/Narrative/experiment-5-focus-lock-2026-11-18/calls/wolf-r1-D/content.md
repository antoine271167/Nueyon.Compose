Central Message: In Yellowstone, wolves are social predators whose kills sustain other animals, while shifts in what they prey on and their effects on elk unfold amid several interacting conditions—not wolf control alone.

Story Progression
- **Context:** Yellowstone’s wolves live in family packs, where members cooperate in caring for young and hunting. Their relationships extend beyond the pack: kills also feed ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.
- **Development:** The prey mix changed over time. In early-winter prey, the proportions represented by elk calves and bull elk reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007; the source links such shifts to prey vulnerability and availability.
- **Endpoint:** Changes in wolf predation and elk impacts are also discussed in relation to other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather. The source says researchers are examining these factors’ relative roles, rather than establishing one as the cause.

Critical Constraints: Do not claim that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes, that the source establishes plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade, or that it quantifies the relative contribution of the interacting factors.

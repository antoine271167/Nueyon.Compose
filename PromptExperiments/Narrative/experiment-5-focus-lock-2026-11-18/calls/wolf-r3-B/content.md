**Selected Story** Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a living network, not a single switch that controls the ecosystem. Their social hunting and the food their kills provide connect them to other animals, while changes in prey and wolf impacts unfold alongside several other factors.

**Story Progression**
- **Context:** Yellowstone wolf packs are social families: they hunt together and cooperate in caring for offspring. Their kills also feed ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.
- **Development:** The balance of elk calves and bull elk in wolves’ early-winter prey changed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source links prey shifts to vulnerability and availability, while also describing wolf predation and elk impacts in relation to other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather.
- **Endpoint:** The Yellowstone account points to a network of relationships and changing conditions—not one predator acting alone. Researchers are examining the factors’ relative roles, which the source does not quantify.

**Critical Constraints** Do not claim that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes, that pack social behavior determines those outcomes, or that the source establishes measured plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade.

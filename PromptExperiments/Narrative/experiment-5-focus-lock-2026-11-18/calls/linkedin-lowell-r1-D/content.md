Lowell’s revival drew on its industrial past, but it did not protect the city from further economic change.

Textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936 as mills closed. For workers, the decline could mean being sent home repeatedly, unsure when work would be available again.

In the 1960s, citizens proposed building on Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage. Political and business leaders backed the effort, and the city council endorsed it in 1972. Heritage and historical parks followed in 1974 and 1978. By the late 1970s and early 1980s, more than 100 old buildings had been adapted for new uses, alongside rising employment and business expansion. The University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories were also part of the city’s 1970s story.

But by the late 1980s, the boom had ended amid a regional slowdown and trouble in the computer industry, including workforce cuts and restructuring at Wang. In the picture captured by a 1992 account, heritage reuse was part of Lowell’s revival—not a shield from the next economic downturn.

Central Message: Yellowstone’s wolves are social predators whose kills feed other animals, while changes in prey and impacts occur amid several interacting factors—not through the work of a lone ecosystem controller.

Story Progression
- **Context:** Yellowstone wolf packs are social families that cooperate in offspring care and group hunting. Their kills also provide food for animals including ravens, coyotes, bears, and invertebrates.
- **Development:** The composition of wolf prey shifted over time: the proportions of early-winter prey made up by elk calves and bull elk reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source links such changes to prey vulnerability and availability.
- **Endpoint:** Changes in wolf predation and elk impacts are described alongside other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather. Research is examining their relative roles, underscoring that the wolf’s effects unfold amid interacting conditions.

Critical Constraints: Do not claim that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes, that pack social behavior determines those outcomes, or that the source establishes plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade. The relative contribution of the interacting factors is not quantified.

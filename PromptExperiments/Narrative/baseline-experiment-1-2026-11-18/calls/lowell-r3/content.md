### Selected Story
Lowell’s shift from textile decline toward heritage-led redevelopment brought renewed activity and new uses for old industrial buildings, but it was not a complete escape from economic change. The city’s preservation effort and the reuse of more than 100 buildings form the clearest evidence of that shift; the downturn by the late 1980s complicates any simple recovery story.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Set the redevelopment against the decline of Lowell’s textile industry: employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936, and one worker recalled repeated spells of being sent home without knowing whether work would be available.
- **Development:** In the 1960s, citizens proposed using Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage in a revitalization strategy. The city council endorsed the idea in 1972, parks followed in 1974 and 1978, and more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated for new uses in the late 1970s and early 1980s, alongside rising employment and business expansion.
- **Endpoint:** By the late 1980s, the boom had ended amid a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry. Lowell’s heritage-led reuse was part of a broader revival, not proof that the city had left economic change behind.

### Critical Constraints
- Frame this as the perspective of a 1992 historical account, not as a current measure of Lowell.
- Do not attribute the revival solely to the parks or preservation effort; the account also identifies the University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories as separate developments of the 1970s.

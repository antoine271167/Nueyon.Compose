In Yellowstone, a wolf’s hunt matters beyond the pack.

Wolves live in social family groups, where they cooperate in caring for young and hunting. Their kills also provide food for ravens, coyotes, bears, and invertebrates.

Their relationship with prey changes, too. Between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in early-winter wolf prey reversed.

That shifting pattern sits within a wider set of conditions: prey availability and vulnerability, other predators, elk management outside the park, and weather. Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a living network, but their impacts are not the work of a single force directing the ecosystem.

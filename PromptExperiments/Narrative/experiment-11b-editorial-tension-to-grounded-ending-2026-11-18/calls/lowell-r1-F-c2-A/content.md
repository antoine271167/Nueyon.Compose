Lowell’s industrial revival took shape while the city was still adjusting to the loss of its textile economy.

Textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936 as mills closed and people left. In the 1960s, Lowell citizens proposed revitalizing the city around its architectural and cultural heritage. The city council endorsed the effort in 1972, and heritage and historical parks followed in 1974 and 1978.

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated for new uses. Employment rose and businesses expanded. The University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories were also part of the city’s changing economic landscape in the 1970s.

But by the late 1980s, a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry—including workforce cuts and restructuring at Wang—had ended the boom.

Reusing Lowell’s industrial heritage was a tangible part of its revival. It was also one chapter in a city’s adaptation to continuing economic change.

### Selected Story
In Yellowstone, wolves are social predators whose relationships extend beyond the pack: they hunt in groups, and their kills provide food for animals ranging from ravens and coyotes to bears and invertebrates. Changes in the prey wolves take—including a reversal in the proportions of elk calves and bull elk among early-winter prey across two periods—add another dimension. The reader should understand that wolf predation and its impacts on elk occur amid interacting conditions, rather than being determined by wolves alone.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Establish Yellowstone wolves as social animals, with cooperative offspring care and group hunting as features of pack life.
- **Development:** Show how wolf kills feed other animals, then use the change in the proportions of elk calves and bull elk among early-winter prey from 1995–2000 to 2001–2007 as a concrete example of changing prey composition.
- **Endpoint:** Place changes in wolf predation and elk impacts in the context of other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather. The central point is that these relationships and conditions complicate any picture of wolves as sole ecosystem controllers.

### Critical Constraints
- Do not imply that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes or that the material establishes an ecosystem-wide cascade or measured plant effects.
- Do not assign a relative contribution or a causal role to any of the factors associated with changes in predation and elk impacts.

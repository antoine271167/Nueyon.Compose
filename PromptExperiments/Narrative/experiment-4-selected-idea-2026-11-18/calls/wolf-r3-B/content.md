**Selected Story** Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a living network, not solitary controllers of the ecosystem: they hunt cooperatively, their kills feed other animals, and the prey they take—and the impacts of predation—change amid several interacting conditions.

**Story Progression**
- **Context:** Yellowstone wolf packs are social families. Cooperative care of offspring and group hunting are among the benefits of this social life.
- **Development:** Wolf kills also provide food for ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates. Meanwhile, the composition of early-winter prey shifted: elk calves and bull elk reversed in relative proportion between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, with prey vulnerability and availability linked to such changes.
- **Endpoint:** These relationships make wolf predation part of a changing web rather than a single chain of control. Other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather also figure in changes to predation and elk impacts; research is examining their relative roles.

**Critical Constraints:** Do not assign quantified contributions to these factors, imply that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes, or claim measured plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade.

**Selected Story** Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a living network, not solitary animals that control an ecosystem on their own: their pack relationships support cooperative care and group hunting, their kills feed other animals, and changing prey patterns unfold alongside other influences.

**Story Progression**
- **Context:** Yellowstone wolf packs are social families. Cooperative offspring care and group hunting are among the benefits of this social living, while wolf kills provide food for animals including ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.
- **Development:** The composition of wolves’ early-winter prey shifted: the relative proportions of elk calves and bull elk reversed between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007. The source links prey changes to vulnerability and availability.
- **Endpoint:** Changes in wolf predation and its impacts on elk are also discussed in relation to other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather. Research is examining their relative roles, making the evidence a story of interacting conditions rather than a single predator directing ecosystem outcomes.

**Critical Constraints:** Do not assign a quantified share of the changes to any factor, claim measured plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade, or imply that pack behavior alone determines outcomes.

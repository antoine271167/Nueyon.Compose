### Selected Story
In Yellowstone, wolves are social predators whose ecological relationships extend beyond their own packs: cooperative care and group hunting shape pack life, while wolf kills provide food for other animals. Changes in the composition of wolf prey offer a concrete example of how predation varies over time. The reader should understand that these relationships and changing predation occur amid other conditions, so wolves alone do not determine ecosystem outcomes.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Introduce Yellowstone wolves as social animals living in family packs, with cooperative offspring care and group hunting among the features of pack life.
- **Development:** Show how wolf kills feed animals ranging from ravens and coyotes to bears and invertebrates. Then use the shift in the proportions of elk calves and bull elk among early-winter prey between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007 as a specific example of changing prey composition, which the source links to prey vulnerability and availability.
- **Endpoint:** Place changing wolf predation and elk impacts alongside other factors, including other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather. Conclude that Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a set of interacting food and social relationships, not solitary controllers of ecosystem outcomes.

### Critical Constraints
- Do not claim that wolves alone determine ecosystem outcomes or that the material establishes an ecosystem-wide cascade or measured plant effects.
- Do not attribute the prey-composition shift or broader changes in predation and elk impacts to pack social behavior alone.

### Selected Story
Lowell’s shift from textile decline toward heritage-led redevelopment brought renewed activity and new uses for old industrial buildings, but it did not mark a lasting escape from economic change. The story follows the citizen-led effort to use the city’s architectural and cultural heritage, the building reuse and broader revival reported in the following decades, and the downturn by the late 1980s. The reader should understand this as a qualified account of redevelopment, not a simple recovery story.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Establish Lowell’s industrial decline through falling textile employment and the insecurity experienced by workers as mills closed.
- **Development:** Describe how citizens proposed using Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage for revitalization in the 1960s, with political and business support and city council endorsement in 1972. Heritage and historical parks followed, while more than 100 old buildings were later rehabilitated for new uses amid rising employment and business expansion.
- **Endpoint:** Close with the late-1980s downturn, amid a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry, showing that heritage reuse was part of a broader revival rather than an escape from economic change.

### Critical Constraints
- Frame the account as a historical perspective from 1992, not a current measurement of Lowell.
- Do not attribute the broader recovery solely to the parks or preservation work; the account also identifies the University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories as separate developments.
- Do not imply that the revival caused the downturn or that the parks, preservation work, university, or Wang independently caused the recovery.

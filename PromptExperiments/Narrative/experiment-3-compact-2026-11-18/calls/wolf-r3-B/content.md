**Selected Story** Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a living network: they hunt and raise young cooperatively, and their kills feed other animals. Their changing prey and impacts, however, unfold alongside other changing conditions—not through the actions of one predator alone.

**Story Progression**
- **Context:** Yellowstone wolf packs are social families. Cooperative care of offspring and group hunting are among the benefits of living socially; wolf kills also provide food for animals including ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.
- **Development:** The prey mix changed over time. In early winter, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in wolf prey were reversed in 2001–2007 compared with 1995–2000. The source links such changes to prey availability and vulnerability, while also identifying other interacting factors in changes to wolf predation and elk impacts.
- **Endpoint:** Yellowstone’s example is a network of relationships and changing conditions, not a simple chain in which wolves dictate ecosystem outcomes. Research is examining the factors’ relative roles, which the source does not establish.

**Critical Constraints** Do not claim that wolves alone control ecosystem outcomes, that the source measures plant effects or an ecosystem-wide cascade, or that it quantifies the relative contribution of the interacting factors.

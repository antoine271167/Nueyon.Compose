A wolf kill in Yellowstone can feed far more than the pack.

Wolves live in social family groups, caring for young and hunting together. Their kills also provide food for ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates—linking wolves to other animals across the landscape.

What wolves prey on can change, too. Between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in early-winter prey reversed. Those shifts are linked to prey vulnerability and availability, while wolf predation and elk impacts also unfold amid other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather.

Yellowstone’s wolves matter through their relationships with pack members, prey and scavengers. Their changing impacts, though, are part of a larger web of interacting conditions—not the work of one predator directing an ecosystem.

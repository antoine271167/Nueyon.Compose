Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a network of relationships—not just predators pursuing prey.

Wolf packs are social families. They hunt together and care for their young cooperatively. Their kills also feed other animals, including ravens, coyotes, bears and invertebrates.

What wolves prey on can shift, too. Between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007, the relative shares of elk calves and bull elk in early-winter prey reversed. Those shifts relate to prey vulnerability and availability, while wolf predation and its impacts on elk occur amid other factors, including other predators, ungulate management outside the park and weather.

Wolves matter through their relationships with pack members, prey and scavengers. But their changing impacts are part of a more complex picture—not the work of a single predator directing the ecosystem.

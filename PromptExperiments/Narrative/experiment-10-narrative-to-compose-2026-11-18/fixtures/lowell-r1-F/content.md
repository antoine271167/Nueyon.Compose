**Selected Story**
Lowell’s shift from textile manufacturing toward heritage-led redevelopment gave old industrial buildings new uses and formed part of a broader revival. But the 1992 historical account also describes a downturn by the late 1980s, complicating any claim that the city had escaped economic change.

**Story Progression**
- **Context:** Textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936 as mills closed and people left. One worker recalled being sent home repeatedly, unsure whether work would be available.
- **Development:** In the 1960s, Lowell citizens proposed building revitalization around the city’s architectural and cultural heritage. Political and business leaders supported the effort, and the city council endorsed it in 1972; heritage and historical parks followed in 1974 and 1978. The account places this effort within a broader revival, then records a reversal by the late 1980s.

**Evidence/Observed Results**
The account reports that more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated for new uses in the late 1970s and early 1980s, alongside rising employment and business expansion. It also identifies the University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories as separate developments of the 1970s. By the late 1980s, a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry—including Wang workforce cuts and restructuring—had ended the boom.

**Editorial Conclusion**
Lowell’s reuse of its industrial heritage was a tangible part of its revival, but not proof of a lasting or single-cause recovery. The later downturn makes the more grounded story one of adaptation amid continuing economic change.

**Critical Constraints**
Treat this as the perspective of a 1992 historical account, not a current assessment. The source does not quantify the separate contributions of parks, preservation, the university, Wang, or other factors, nor establish whether the revival endured after the late-1980s downturn.

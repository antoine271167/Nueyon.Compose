### Selected Story
Lowell’s shift from textile decline toward heritage-based redevelopment brought new uses to its industrial buildings, but it was part of a broader and incomplete revival. The reader should understand both the citizen-led preservation effort and the reported building reuse and renewed activity, then see that this period was followed by a downturn by the late 1980s—not a permanent escape from economic change.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Lowell’s textile employment fell sharply amid mill closures and departures; one worker recalled being sent home repeatedly without knowing whether work would be available.
- **Development:** In the 1960s, citizens proposed using Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage as part of revitalization. Political and business leaders supported the effort, the city council endorsed it in 1972, and heritage and historical parks followed. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, more than 100 old buildings were rehabilitated for new uses alongside rising employment and business expansion.
- **Endpoint:** By the late 1980s, the boom had ended amid a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry, including Wang Laboratories’ workforce cuts and restructuring. Lowell’s reuse of its industrial past was one part of a broader revival, not an escape from subsequent economic change.

### Critical Constraints
- Frame the account as a historical perspective from 1992, not a description of Lowell today.
- Do not attribute the broader revival solely to the parks or preservation effort; the account also identifies the University of Lowell and Wang Laboratories as separate developments of the 1970s.

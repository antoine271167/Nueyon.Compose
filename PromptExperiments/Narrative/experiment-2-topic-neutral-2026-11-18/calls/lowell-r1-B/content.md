### Selected Story
Lowell’s shift from textile manufacturing toward heritage-based reuse took place against a long industrial decline, but it was not a complete escape from economic change. A civic effort to make use of the city’s architectural and cultural heritage was followed by parks and the rehabilitation of more than 100 old buildings for new uses, alongside rising employment and business expansion. The later downturn shows that this redevelopment was part of a broader revival, not a simple or lasting recovery story.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Set out Lowell’s industrial decline: textile employment fell from 17,000 in 1895 to 8,000 by 1936, with mill closures and uncertainty for workers.
- **Development:** Describe citizens’ proposal in the 1960s to use Lowell’s architectural and cultural heritage in revitalization, followed by political and business support, city council endorsement in 1972, parks in 1974 and 1978, and the reuse of more than 100 buildings in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Employment and business expansion rose during this period as well.
- **Endpoint:** Bring the story to the late-1980s downturn, when the boom had ended amid a regional slowdown and difficulties in the computer industry. The contrast leaves the reader with a revival that included heritage reuse but did not put Lowell beyond economic change.

### Critical Constraints
- Do not present the parks or heritage reuse as the sole cause of the broader revival; employment and business expansion occurred alongside them, and other developments also contributed to the period.
- Do not present the University of Lowell or Wang Laboratories as sole causes of recovery.
- Treat this as the perspective of a 1992 historical account, not as a measurement of Lowell’s current conditions.

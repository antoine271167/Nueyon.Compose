### Selected Story
Yellowstone’s wolves are part of a network of social and food relationships, rather than solitary predators that determine ecosystem outcomes on their own. Their packs cooperate in raising offspring and hunting, and their kills feed animals ranging from ravens and coyotes to bears and invertebrates. A shift in the composition of their early-winter prey offers a concrete example of changing predation amid several interacting conditions.

### Story Progression
- **Context:** Introduce Yellowstone wolves as social animals that live in family packs, with cooperative offspring care and group hunting.
- **Development:** Show how wolf kills provide food for other animals, then use the reversal in the proportions of elk calves and bull elk among early-winter prey between 1995–2000 and 2001–2007 as an example of changing prey composition. The source links such changes to prey vulnerability and availability.
- **Endpoint:** Place changing predation and elk impacts alongside other predators, ungulate management outside the park, and weather. The central point is that wolves participate in a network of relationships, while ecosystem outcomes are not attributable to wolves alone.

### Critical Constraints
- Do not claim that wolves alone control Yellowstone’s ecosystem or that the source establishes an ecosystem-wide cascade or measured plant effects.
- Do not present the listed factors as quantified causes of the changes; their relative roles are not established here.
